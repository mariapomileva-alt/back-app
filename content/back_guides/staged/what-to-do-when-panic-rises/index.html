The next minute

When panic rises, choose one small thing for the next minute.

You do not need to solve the whole feeling at once. The only job is to find one action that feels possible now.

Back Guides · 6 minute read

A rush of panic can make every thought feel urgent. Your body may feel fast, strange, or hard to read; your mind may ask for an answer immediately. A useful first move is not to argue with every thought. It is to make the next minute smaller.

If something feels medically urgent, treat it as urgent.

This guide cannot tell whether physical symptoms are caused by panic or something else. If symptoms are new, severe, worsening, or make you think you may need urgent medical help, contact your local emergency service. If you feel at risk of harming yourself or someone else, seek immediate crisis or emergency support.

Start with permission, not a test

You do not need to perform calmness correctly. You do not need to prove that you can breathe a certain way, sit still, or think positively. Try saying something less demanding:

“This is a hard moment. I only need one small next step.”

That sentence does not promise a particular outcome. It simply gives you less to carry right now.

A three-part next-minute plan

  1. Notice one fact around you. Name a colour, feel the support beneath your feet, or look for the edge of a doorway. Choose one concrete detail rather than scanning everything.
  2. Choose one gentle tool. You might take an unforced breath, press your feet into the floor, hold a cool glass, listen to a steady sound, or give your attention a small task. If one option feels wrong, you can switch.
  3. Choose your support route. Stay where you are, move to a quieter place if that feels safer, or contact someone you trust. Back’s Call my person option can open your phone’s call or message app; you remain in control of whether anything is sent.

Let the tool be small

People often feel pressure to find the perfect technique. There is no prize for the most elaborate response. A small action can be enough to interrupt the demand that you must fix everything right now.

In Back, the six tools are different on purpose. Breathe may feel useful one day; Ground, Move, Listen, Read, or a simple distraction may be a better fit another day. You can stop or change tools at any time.

When the minute is over

If the feeling has shifted even a little, you do not have to turn that into a big achievement. You can have some water, sit somewhere comfortable, or write down one thing that made the moment more manageable.

If the feeling is not easing, keeps returning, or starts to interfere with daily life, more support can help. A health professional can help you understand what is happening and discuss appropriate care. Reaching out is not a failure of self-help.
