A gentler route

When breathing feels hard during panic, do not force it.

Breathing exercises are optional. If paying close attention to your breath makes the moment feel bigger, choose another way to orient.

Back Guides · 5 minute read

When anxiety or panic is high, being asked to control your breath can feel like one more thing to get right. For some people, counting or taking a deep breath is helpful. For others, it makes breathing feel more noticeable or uncomfortable. Both responses are valid.

Do not assume breathlessness is panic.

Breathing difficulty can have many causes. This guide cannot assess symptoms. If breathing feels severely difficult, is new or worsening, or you think you may need urgent medical help, contact local emergency services.

You are allowed to leave breathing alone

A support tool should make the next moment more possible, not add pressure. If breathing is not the right entry point today, you can choose something outside the breath instead:

  • Feel the weight of both feet against the floor.
  • Look for three edges or shapes in the room.
  • Hold an object with a clear texture.
  • Listen for one steady sound and let the rest be there.
  • Move your shoulders, hands, or toes gently if movement feels okay.

If you do want to include breath

Make it less of a performance. Instead of trying to take a dramatic deep breath, notice the breath you already have. You might let the exhale be a little less rushed, or place a hand somewhere comfortable and simply feel a small movement. There is no required count and no need to continue if it feels worse.

A useful rule: if a tool asks too much of you, make it smaller or choose another one.

Switching is a skill, not giving up

Back offers Breathe alongside Ground, Move, Listen, Read, and Distract because one kind of support will not suit every nervous system or every moment. You can begin with Breathe and move away from it. You can skip it entirely. The goal is not to complete a sequence; it is to find a little more room for the next choice.

Make a note for a calmer time

Later, when the intensity has passed, it can help to remember what felt neutral, helpful, or unhelpful. A simple note such as “counting was too much; looking around helped” gives you a more personal starting point next time.
