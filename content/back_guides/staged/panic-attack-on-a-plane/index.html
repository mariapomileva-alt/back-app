Panic Attack on a Plane

You do not have to finish the flight in your head. The next small choice is enough.

Back Guides · 5 minute read

If a panic attack starts on a plane, you do not have to leave your seat or look calm. Feel the seat under you, hold one object, and pick one quiet next step. Tell the crew if you want help or if something feels medically urgent. This guide cannot say what is happening in your body.

Do not wait out a symptom that feels urgent.

This guide cannot assess chest pain, breathlessness, faintness, or any other physical symptom. If symptoms are new, severe, worsening, or make you think you may need urgent medical help, tell a crew member and ask for medical assistance. If you feel at risk of harming yourself or someone else, seek immediate help from the crew.

Use the seat you already have

A plane gives you fewer options, and that can feel like pressure. You can still make the space a little simpler. Press both feet into the floor. Feel the seat against your back. Hold the armrest or a bag strap. Loosen anything at your neck if that feels okay. You do not have to stand up.

Give attention one quiet job

If a breathing count makes the moment louder, leave it. You might look at one colour on the seat in front of you, notice a steady engine sound, or name the shape of your hands. Headphones with a familiar sound can help some people. Silence can help others. Either is allowed.

You can switch. If a tool, a count, or a thought makes this harder, stop and choose a smaller thing.

Ask for help in one short line

The crew are there for safety, including medical worry. You can say: “I am having a hard moment. Could I have water?” or “I think I may need medical help.” A person beside you can stay quiet, hold a hand if you want that, or press the call button for you. You do not owe them a story.

If you have a phone tool

Back works offline and does not need an account, so it can still open in aeroplane mode. Ground, Listen, or a simple distraction may feel more possible than breath. You can pause or leave at any time. A tool is optional, not a test.

After landing, or later in the flight

You might drink water, sit still, or tell someone you trust when you can. If flying panic keeps returning or is getting in the way of travel you need, a qualified health professional can help you think through more support. That conversation can wait until you are on the ground.
