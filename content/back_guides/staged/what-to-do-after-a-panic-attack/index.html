What to Do After a Panic Attack: A Gentle Next Step

After a panic attack, you may feel tired, shaky, relieved, embarrassed, or simply not ready to talk about it. There is no correct way to feel afterwards. The next useful thing can be very ordinary: sit somewhere quieter, have a drink, eat if you want to, or let the day become smaller for a while.

You do not have to turn a hard moment into a lesson before you have had a chance to come back to yourself.

Back Guides · 4 minute read

It can take time for your body and attention to feel more settled after a surge of fear. You are allowed to keep the next hour simple. The aim is not to prove that you are fine. It is to notice what feels possible now.

Do not assume symptoms are only panic.

This guide cannot assess chest pain, breathlessness, faintness, or any other physical symptom. If symptoms are new, severe, worsening, or make you think you may need urgent medical help, contact your local emergency service. If you are in immediate danger or at risk of harm, seek emergency or crisis support now.

Give yourself a little less to do

If you can, postpone anything that does not need an answer right now. Sit down, change rooms, take a slower route home, or let someone else handle a small practical thing. A panic attack can be exhausting. Resting is not avoidance; sometimes it is simply what the next part of the day needs.

Return to ordinary needs

You might want water, food, a shower, fresh air, or a familiar show. You might not. Try not to make a list of things you should do. One ordinary need is enough to notice. If you are around someone you trust, you can ask for quiet company without having to explain every detail.

Tell one person, if that would help

A short message can be enough: “I had a hard moment. I am safe, but I could use a check-in later.” Or you can say nothing for now. If you do want to talk, it may help to share what kind of response feels useful: company, practical help, or simply someone listening. Back can open your phone’s call or message screen for a saved contact when you choose; it never sends a message or makes a call by itself.

Notice, but do not interrogate the moment

Later, when you have more space, you might make one gentle note: where you were, what felt difficult, or what gave you even a little room. You do not need to find a cause or write a complete account. One small observation can help you recognise a pattern over time — and you can leave it alone if reflecting makes the day feel louder.

Keep a small option close for next time

You might save one line that helped, a trusted person to contact, or one Back tool you would be willing to try again. Breathe, Distract, Ground, Move, Listen, and Read are choices, not a routine you have to complete. You can open one, switch, or close the app.

When more support belongs in the picture

If panic or anxiety keeps returning, is getting worse, or is making everyday life harder, it can help to speak with a qualified health professional. That is not a failure of self-help. It is another route to support. You can bring a few notes about what has been happening, or begin with only one sentence.
