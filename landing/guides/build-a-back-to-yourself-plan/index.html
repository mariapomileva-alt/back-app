A plan you can change

Build a Personal Back-to-Yourself Plan

Updated 2026-11-06

Written by Back Guides Editorial Team

A plan does not have to predict a difficult moment or tell you exactly what to do. It can simply hold a few choices you have already made on a steadier day: something small that might help, someone you might contact, and what you will do if you need more support.

Think of it as a small menu, not a test. You can change it, skip it, or choose only one part.

Back Guides · 5 minute read

It can be hard to decide what to do when you are already overwhelmed. A personal plan gives you somewhere ordinary to start. You do not need to make it complete, clever, or permanent. A few words that sound like you are enough.

A personal plan is not a substitute for urgent help.

This guide cannot assess symptoms or tell you whether a situation is an emergency. If you think you may need urgent medical help, contact your local emergency service. If you are in immediate danger or at risk of harm, seek emergency or crisis support now.

Begin with the smallest question

Instead of asking “What will fix this?”, try “What could make the next few minutes a little more possible?” Your answer might be sitting somewhere quieter, putting both feet on the floor, drinking some water, opening a familiar sound, reading a short reminder, or sending one simple message. It does not need to work every time to belong on the list.

Make three small sections

You might keep your plan to three headings:

  • Things I could try on my own: two or three gentle options, not a long routine.
  • People or places I could turn to: a trusted person, a local support route, or a place where you tend to feel less alone.
  • When I need more help: a clear reminder to contact a health professional, urgent service, or emergency support when the situation feels unsafe or symptoms are worrying.

Those headings leave room for a real life, not an ideal one. You can write “maybe” beside every option if that feels kinder.

Choose language you would actually believe

A plan can include one short line to come back to. “One thing at a time.” “I can ask for company.” “I do not have to decide everything now.” Borrowing words is fine, but changing them until they sound like your own can make them easier to return to.

Let a tool be one option, not the whole plan

Back can sit in the first section of your menu: Breathe, Distract, Ground, Move, Listen, or Read. You can open one tool, switch to another, or close the app. If you save a trusted contact, Back can open your phone’s call or message screen when you choose; it never contacts anyone by itself. You can also keep another support route on your plan that has nothing to do with an app.

Put it somewhere you will find it

A note in a drawer, a saved card on your phone, or a short list in a familiar app can all work. The useful place is not necessarily the most visible one — it is the one you can reach without looking for it. You can make a private version for yourself and a shorter version to share with someone you trust.

Revisit it after a real day

After a difficult moment has passed, you might notice that one part helped and another felt like pressure. Cross out what no longer fits. Add something small you wish had been there. If anxiety, panic, or overwhelm is recurring, worsening, or getting in the way of daily life, talking with a qualified health professional can be an important part of your wider support plan.
