When You Cannot Decide What to Do: Start Smaller

Updated 2026-10-27

Written by Back Guides Editorial Team

When your mind is crowded, even a helpful list can become one more decision. You do not have to choose the best tool or work out the whole day. Pick one small thing that asks almost nothing: feel your feet, look at one ordinary object, sit down, or send one short message.

The aim is not to find a perfect answer. It is to make the next choice small enough to reach.

Back Guides · 4 minute read

Strong anxiety, panic, exhaustion, or overwhelm can make ordinary choices feel unusually heavy. Should you breathe? Move? Text someone? Leave? Stay? If nothing feels clearly right, that does not mean you are failing. It may mean the decision has become too large for this minute.

Do not assume a new or severe symptom is anxiety or panic.

This guide cannot assess symptoms or tell you whether you are having an emergency. If you think you may need urgent medical help, contact your local emergency service. If you are in immediate danger or at risk of harm, seek emergency or crisis support now.

Make the question smaller

Instead of asking, “What should I do?”, try a question with only one small answer: “What can I notice for ten seconds?” “Where can I put my body?” “What is one sound I can hear?” You do not need to solve what caused the feeling or decide what happens later. The next minute is enough to work with.

Choose from a tiny menu

A menu of two or three options can be gentler than an open-ended choice. You could pick one of these:

  • rest your hands on a solid surface;
  • look for one neutral colour or shape nearby;
  • sit, lean against a wall, or put both feet on the floor;
  • listen for one steady sound; or
  • read one familiar sentence.

If one option feels irritating or impossible, let it go. Choosing a different option is not starting over.

Let the body make one practical choice

Sometimes a practical adjustment is the most available step: loosen a tight layer, take a sip of water, move to a quieter edge of a room, or sit where your back is supported. There is no need to turn this into an exercise. It can simply make the moment a little easier to inhabit.

Use a sentence that does not demand certainty

You might quietly say, “I only need the next minute,” or “I can choose one thing.” If you want support, the sentence can be just as small: “Can you stay with me for a moment?” You do not have to explain the feeling perfectly before you ask.

Let Back hold the menu, not the answer

When more support could help

If anxiety or panic is repeatedly making daily life hard, or the small things are not helping you cope, consider speaking with a qualified health professional. Support can help you understand what is happening and talk through options that fit your circumstances.
